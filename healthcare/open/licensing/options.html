Licensing options memo

These are drafts prepared for counsel. They are not in effect, and no licence has been granted.

0. The goal, stated precisely

Adam's goal, as recorded in the design: give the open part of the blueHealthcare clinical platform to health systems worldwide that cannot pay, while the company sells the commercial blueHealthcare product line (US first, then Germany, Australia, Saudi Arabia, UK) (the design/00the design/the strategic plan, "commitment #4" and the strategic-paths table, path 4). The mechanism the design already names is open-core: "the receipt spine and verifier are open. The healthcare applications ship a community edition under a public-sector / low-income-country license with a scoped patent grant (open-core, the DHIS2/OpenMRS model)" (the strategic plan, commitment 4).

This memo's first job is to correct a framing problem in that sentence before comparing options, because it shapes every option below.

0.1 An OSI open-source licence cannot do what "public-sector / low-income-country license" implies

The Open Source Definition (OSD), maintained by the Open Source Initiative, is the standard the industry and OpenMRS/DHIS2 use to decide what counts as "open source." Two of its ten criteria are directly relevant here:

  • "No Discrimination Against Persons or Groups" — "The license must not discriminate against any

    • person or group of persons."

  • "No Discrimination Against Fields of Endeavor" — "The license must not restrict anyone from

    • making use of the program in a specific field of endeavor," including business use. (Open Source Initiative, The Open Source Definition: https://opensource.org/osd, verified live.)

    Put plainly: an OSI-approved open-source licence cannot be scoped to "low-income countries" or "health systems that can't pay." It cannot charge based on ability to pay, cannot exclude a specific class of user (including a large, well-funded hospital chain or a commercial vendor), and cannot be field-of-use-restricted to the public sector. A licence that tried to do any of those things would not be an open-source licence by the OSD's own test, and could not credibly borrow the "open source" label that makes the OpenMRS/DHIS2 comparison valuable in the first place.

    How "free for those who can't pay" is actually achieved, then, is not a clause in the outbound licence — it is the combination of (a) a genuinely open licence on the spine, which is free for everyone including a well-resourced buyer, plus (b) a separate commercial offering (support, hosting, the proprietary application layer, integrations, SLAs) that the company sells to those who can pay. That is exactly what "open core" means in practice, and it is what OpenMRS and DHIS2 do: their core software is unconditionally open to everyone, and revenue comes from implementation partners, hosting, and paid modules/services layered on top — not from a licence clause that reads income.

    0.2 Why source-available licences (BSL, Elastic License, PolyForm) are a different category, not a variant

    A "source-available" licence publishes the source but limits who may run it in production, or requires a fee once usage crosses some line — that is precisely a field-of-use or identity restriction, which is what the OSD forbids. Concretely:

  • Business Source License (BSL/BUSL): source is published, but a licensee is barred from certain

    • production uses (defined per release) until a stated "Change Date," after which it automatically converts to a real open-source licence (commonly Apache-2.0 or GPL). It is not OSI-approved and does not meet the OSD. (Wikipedia, "Business Source License": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BusinessSource_License>; MariaDB's own BSL FAQ describes the same mechanic: https://mariadb.com/bsl-faq-adopting/.)

  • Elastic License: source-available, free to use, but with use restrictions (notably barring

    • competing "as a service" offerings); after a period it can convert to Apache-2.0. Elastic's own blog describes the change from Apache-2.0 to this model and back again over time. (Elastic, "License Change Clarification": https://www.elastic.co/blog/license-change-clarification.)

  • PolyForm licences: a family of template source-available licences (noncommercial, small-business,

    • internal-use, shield, etc.), each with an explicit field-of-use or competitor restriction by design. (Overview via FOSSA's guide: https://fossa.com/blog/comprehensive-guide-source-available-software-licenses/.)

    OSI describes this category of licence as failing the Open Source Definition and has publicly used the term "fauxpen" for source-available licences marketed as if they were open source (summarized in the FOSSA guide above; the exact OSI usage of "fauxpen" and its original source were not independently fetched and should be treated as unverified — confirm before quoting it to counsel).

    Why this matters for blueOpenHealthcare specifically: a source-available licence would look, on its face, like it satisfies "give it to those who can't pay, keep it from those who can" — but it does that by exactly the mechanism the OSD forbids, so the project could not call the result "open source," could not credibly claim the OpenMRS/DHIS2 comparison, and — separately — a "cannot compete with us" clause is the one thing large vendors have the least trouble ignoring in low-oversight jurisdictions, because it is a contract term to be litigated, not a permission that was never granted. It buys reputational cost, not real protection, and it costs the open-source narrative that is the whole point of the international-access strategy. It is included in the comparison table below for completeness, and is not recommended.

    2. Why OpenMRS and Bahmni matter as comparators

    the design explicitly names "the DHIS2/OpenMRS model" as the target (the strategic plan commitment 4), and the strategy row for the community edition names an OpenMRS/Bahmni module as the first integration target. Verified current facts:

  • OpenMRS: MPL-2.0 with an added Health-Related Additional Disclaimer of Warranty and Limitation

    • of Liability. History: OpenMRS Public License 1.0 (from-scratch) → a modified MPL 1.1 → MPL 2.0, the last move made specifically with the OSI's help to land on a real, OSI-approved licence rather than a bespoke one (OpenMRS wiki, "Proposed Adoption of MPL 2.0 with Disclaimer": https://openmrs.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/RES/pages/26312431/Proposed+Adoption+of+MPL+2.0+with+Disclaimer; license file: https://github.com/openmrs/openmrs-core/blob/master/LICENSE). OpenMRS does not require the same licence for community-contributed modules (OpenMRS wiki, "Licensing of OpenMRS Modules FAQ": https://openmrs.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/docs/pages/26312765/Licensing+of+OpenMRS+Modules+FAQ).

  • Bahmni: AGPL-3.0 today (https://www.bahmni.org/license, https://www.bahmni.org/license-faq),

    • with an active 2025 community proposal to move to MPL-2.0 (matching OpenMRS) or MIT for some repositories (https://talk.openmrs.org/t/proposal-for-license-change-for-bahmni-requesting-community-inputs-by-nov-15-2025/47267).

  • DHIS2 (mentioned in the design as a second reference open-core project): the dhis2-core

    • repository's LICENSE file is BSD-3-Clause, not AGPL (verified by fetching https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dhis2/dhis2-core/master/LICENSE directly). This is worth flagging to counsel because the design phrase "DHIS2/OpenMRS model" implies a shared approach, but the two projects use materially different licences (BSD-3-Clause, a permissive licence with no copyleft, versus OpenMRS's file-level-copyleft MPL-2.0). DHIS2's own web terms (https://dhis2.org/terms-privacy/) were checked and cover only the dhis2.org website, not the software licence — the BSD-3-Clause finding above is the software licence. DHIS2 also runs a separate, more actively governed brand-control document for its name and marks; a specific DHIS2 trademark-policy URL was searched for and not found in a form that could be verified — mark DHIS2's trademark practice as unverified (see the trademark policy).

    Implication for counsel: "the DHIS2/OpenMRS model" is really two different licensing choices (BSD-3-Clause vs. MPL-2.0) united only by the open-core business model (open engine, revenue from services/paid layers on top). Picking a single outbound licence to "match" both is not possible; counsel should decide whether OpenMRS's MPL-2.0 (closer fit: healthcare-specific disclaimer precedent, and the module most likely to be extended — Bahmni — may itself be moving to MPL-2.0) or a permissive licence (closer fit to DHIS2's actual choice) better serves the stated goal.

    3. Export control and sanctions — flagged, not resolved

    This program is a global release, and the strategy explicitly includes low-income and Gulf-region markets. Two distinct questions exist and neither is resolved here:

    1. Is the software itself subject to U.S. export control (EAR) at all, and does an open-source release change that? Publicly available, freely downloadable open-source encryption source code is generally excluded from EAR licensing requirements once a required notification is filed, under License Exception TSU (15 C.F.R. § 740.13(e)); the Linux Foundation has published general guidance on how U.S. export control interacts with open-source projects (https://www.linuxfoundation.org/resources/publications/understanding-us-export-controls-with-open-source-projects; BIS's own encryption-controls page: https://www.bis.gov/learn-support/encryption-controls/encryption-items-not-subject-to-ear). blueHealthcare's receipt/verifier spine signs records with public-key cryptography (Ed25519, per the project's design's description of the security page), which is exactly the kind of thing this exception was written for — but whether the specific notification has been filed, and whether any non-encryption part of the platform is separately controlled, is a question only counsel (with export-control expertise) can answer. Not resolved here. 2. Sanctions / restricted-party screening for who may download or contribute. An OSI licence cannot restrict who uses the software — but U.S. sanctions law is a separate, mandatory overlay that applies regardless of licence choice: the company likely cannot knowingly make the software available to, or accept contributions that route through, sanctioned countries, entities, or listed individuals, irrespective of what the licence says. GitHub itself, as a US company, already restricts access from certain sanctioned jurisdictions at the platform level, which may do some of this work by default — but that is a fact about GitHub's own policy, not a substitute for the company's own OFAC/BIS compliance analysis. Not resolved here; flagged as Q9 in legal guidance.

    4. Recommendation for counsel to test

    Recommendation (not a decision): license the core/ (the receipt spine, verifier, record core, and jurisdiction interfaces) under MPL-2.0, following OpenMRS's own precedent of adding a healthcare-specific warranty/liability disclaimer on top, and keep app/ (billing, tenancy, enterprise exports, and the commercial healthcare applications) proprietary and unlicensed for public distribution — i.e., go with the open-core row of the table above, using MPL-2.0 as the specific licence for the open half. MPL-2.0's file-level copyleft gives real protection against a vendor quietly forking the spine into a closed SaaS without giving anything back, it explicitly permits combination with proprietary code in a "Larger Work" so app/ is never at legal risk of being pulled under the open licence, it carries an explicit patent grant, and it is the licence the project's own most likely integration partner (OpenMRS) already uses — which lowers friction if a module is ever contributed back and forth between the two projects. AGPL-3.0 is the strongest alternative to test if counsel weighs SaaS-forking risk more heavily than combination flexibility; it is also Bahmni's current (though possibly transitional) licence, which matters if Bahmni integration specifically is the priority over OpenMRS integration.
