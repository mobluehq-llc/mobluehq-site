blueOpenHealthcare

blueOpenHealthcare is the free, source-available edition of blueHealthcare, priced by country using the World Bank's own published income classification rather than a bespoke list: free automatically for low- and lower-middle-income countries, free case by case for upper-middle-income countries, and licensed commercially everywhere else. It exists for two reasons, not one — so that health systems that cannot pay for software can still run a signed, verifiable clinical record, and because it is a channel to developers and clinicians who already move between two health systems and can teach us what their country's coding, privacy law and language actually require. No patient data, ever — real or "anonymised" — anywhere in this community.

At a glance: this edition vs. the commercial platform

blueOpenHealthcare — this section

  • Source-available; the licence restricts who may run it for free, so it is not "open source"
  • Free for low- and lower-middle-income countries; case-by-case for upper-middle-income; a commercial licence is required everywhere else
  • Running it for real patients requires sharing back changes and translations — never patient data
  • Code contributions are not yet accepted — the licence is proposed and pending legal review
  • A lean, growing subset of the platform's modules (see "What's in the free edition" below)

The commercial blueHealthcare platform

  • Licensed commercially, sold by contract — see the healthcare overview
  • Available to any organisation regardless of country, including high-income countries such as the US, Canada, the UK, Germany, Australia, and Saudi Arabia
  • No share-back obligation
  • The full product suite listed on the overview page

Both editions run the same signed-receipt architecture; a named human remains the only decision authority in either one.

Who this is for

Eligibility is set by the World Bank's country income classification, published annually: low- and lower-middle-income countries are eligible automatically, upper-middle-income countries may apply and MOBLUEHQ decides case by case, and high-income countries (Canada, the US, the UK, Germany, Australia, Saudi Arabia among them) need a commercial licence. It's free to use, provided you share back what you build. Anyone, anywhere, may read the code and build with synthetic data — the location test applies only to running it for real patients.

This exists for two reasons, not one: so that health systems that cannot pay for software can still run a signed, verifiable clinical record, and because it's how the software reaches developers and clinicians who already move between two health systems — exactly the people best placed to tell us what a country's coding, privacy law and language actually require. Share-back means registering each live clinic and sending back the code of your changes and translations — never patient data. See the full eligibility and share-back terms.

Canada, the US, Europe, and other high-income countries: contact us for a commercial licence.

What's in the free edition

A lean list, expanded as it goes:

  • Included now: patient record and terminology, registration and scheduling, clinical notes, the launcher, the receipt verifier (open to everyone), and a developer kit — synthetic demo data, a sandbox, and translation and country templates.
  • Being prepared for release: the rest of the clinical platform — orders, results, medicines, lab, imaging, and integrations — together with blueChart, blueClaims, blueBench, blueDelta, blueRecall, blueNull, and blueCatch, plus the platform's cryptographic eSource module for clinical trials (see the healthcare overview for the current full list). All of it is going through a safety and security review — the same prerequisites as the rest of the platform, plus a per-product check for anything that could mislead a clinician or harm a patient in a country new to it — before any of this code is published. Nothing here is downloadable today: no package, no repository access, no installer.

Order-entry decision support is not a regulated or validated medical device in any country. Drug-interaction checking will show as unavailable until a clinic connects its own licensed drug-interaction knowledge base — nothing here ships invented pharmacology.

Eligibility and the licence itself are proposed and pending legal review — see eligibility and share-back.
